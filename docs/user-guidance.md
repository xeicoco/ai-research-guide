## Who This Is For

This guide is for anyone who uses an AI chatbot, assistant, or research tool and wants to:

- Get more accurate, relevant, and useful answers.
- Recognize when an answer is likely wrong or unreliable.
- Understand the limits of what AI can and cannot do.
- Protect themselves from being misled by AI-generated content.

No technical background is assumed.

## Getting Better Answers: How to Ask

The quality of an AI's answer depends significantly on how the question is asked. The following strategies consistently improve results.

### Be specific

| Vague question | More specific version |
|---|---|
| "Tell me about climate change." | "Summarize the three main mechanisms by which CO2 increases global average temperature." |
| "What should I do about my back pain?" | "What are the evidence-based first-line treatments for non-specific lower back pain that has lasted less than 4 weeks?" |
| "Explain AI." | "Explain, in plain language for a non-technical audience, what a large language model is and how it generates text." |

### Provide context

Include relevant background that helps the AI give a targeted answer:

- Your level of expertise: "I'm a first-year biology student…"
- The purpose: "…I need to explain this in a 5-minute presentation."
- Constraints: "…in fewer than 300 words" or "…without using jargon."

### Ask for structure

If you need a comprehensive answer, ask for it explicitly:

- "Give me a structured overview with pros and cons."
- "Walk me through this step by step."
- "Summarize the key points in bullet form, then explain each one."

### Ask for uncertainty acknowledgment

Explicitly invite the AI to flag what it does not know:

- "If you're uncertain about anything, please say so."
- "Let me know if your information might be outdated."
- "Flag any claims that you can't verify."

### Break complex questions into parts

Instead of asking one large, multi-part question, break it into focused sub-questions. This makes it easier to identify where an answer is strong or weak.

### Challenge the AI's assumptions

If the AI seems to be making an assumption you didn't intend, push back:

- "You assumed I meant X — I actually meant Y. Please redo the answer."
- "I'm not sure your premise is correct. Can you reconsider?"

## Evaluating AI Answers: What to Look For

After receiving an AI answer, apply these checks before acting on it.

### Does it actually answer your question?

Read the answer and ask: "Did the AI answer what I asked, or did it drift to a related but different question?" If it drifted, rephrase and ask again.

### Is it specific and concrete?

Good answers include specific facts, figures, mechanisms, and examples. Vague, general answers ("AI is widely used in many industries…") are a warning sign that the AI may not have useful knowledge on the topic.

### Are claims supported?

For any factual claim that matters:

- Ask the AI to cite its source.
- Verify the citation independently (look it up via a search engine or academic database).
- Remember: **plain LLMs can fabricate citations**. A plausible-sounding reference is not a verified one.

### Does it acknowledge uncertainty?

Good AI answers hedge appropriately:

- "Most sources agree…" or "There is ongoing debate about…"
- "As of my training cutoff in [date], the situation was…"
- "I'm not certain about this — I'd recommend verifying with [source type]."

An answer that is uniformly confident, with no hedging on any point, is suspicious — real research always has some uncertainty.

### Is it recent enough?

All AI systems have a training cutoff date. For time-sensitive topics (current events, recent science, changing regulations, live prices), the AI's answer may be outdated. Always check:

- When was the AI's knowledge cutoff?
- Has this topic changed since then?
- Can you verify with a current source?

## Recognizing Common Problems

### Hallucination

The AI states something confidently that is false. This is most likely when:

- You ask about obscure, specialized, or recent topics.
- You ask for specific statistics, citations, or quotes.
- The question has a plausible-sounding answer that is easy to generate.

**What to do:** Verify any important factual claim with an authoritative external source.

### Fabricated citations

The AI provides a reference (author, title, journal, year) that doesn't exist. This is a specific and common form of hallucination.

**What to do:** Look up every citation before relying on it. Search by DOI, title, or author in a credible academic database (Google Scholar, PubMed, CrossRef, etc.).

### Shallow or padded answers

The AI generates a long, fluent response that says very little. Common signals:

- Lots of qualifiers ("it depends", "there are many factors") without specifying what they are.
- Repeated paraphrasing of the same point.
- Lists of bullet points without explanation.

**What to do:** Ask for more depth — "Explain the first point in detail" or "Give me a concrete example of each."

### Overconfident outdated information

The AI presents stale information as current fact.

**What to do:** For any time-sensitive topic, explicitly ask "What is your knowledge cutoff, and might this information be outdated?" and verify with a current source.

### Agreeing with false premises

If you include a false or questionable assumption in your question, the AI may simply accept it and build on it.

**Example:** "Given that vitamin C cures the common cold, what's the best dose?" — The AI may answer the dosage question without challenging the premise.

**What to do:** Ask the AI directly: "Is the premise of my question correct?"

## When to Seek Other Sources

AI systems are useful for many tasks but are not the right tool for everything. Seek authoritative human expertise or primary sources when:

| Situation | Why |
|---|---|
| **Medical decisions** | AI is not a substitute for diagnosis or treatment by a qualified healthcare provider. |
| **Legal advice** | Laws vary by jurisdiction and change frequently; AI cannot provide legal advice. |
| **Financial decisions** | AI cannot access real-time market data and cannot be held accountable for financial advice. |
| **Safety-critical information** | Engineering tolerances, chemical safety, emergency procedures — always verify with official sources. |
| **Recent events** | AI has a knowledge cutoff; for current events, use a news source or search engine. |
| **Specialized academic claims** | AI may hallucinate citations; use academic databases for primary literature. |
| **High-stakes unique situations** | The more unique and consequential your situation, the less likely a general AI answer applies. |

## Interacting Safely with AI Systems

### Protect your privacy

- Do not share personal identifying information, financial details, passwords, or confidential business information with AI systems unless you understand how that data is stored and used.
- Read the privacy policy of the AI service you are using.
- Assume that conversation content may be used for model improvement unless the service explicitly states otherwise.

### Recognize AI-generated content in the wild

AI-generated content is increasingly common in emails, articles, social media, and other contexts. When evaluating any content:

- Is it unusually fluent but oddly generic?
- Does it lack specific verifiable detail?
- Do citations check out?
- Is the claimed expertise consistent with the stated source?

### Recognize social engineering

AI systems (or people pretending to be AI) can be used to build false trust and manipulate users. Red flags:

- An AI that claims to be human when directly asked.
- An AI that claims credentials it cannot verify (doctor, lawyer, financial advisor).
- An AI that creates urgency or pressure to act quickly.
- An AI that discourages you from seeking a second opinion.

When in doubt, verify with independent sources.

## Quick Reference Card

**Before asking:**
- Make the question specific and provide context.
- Break complex questions into parts.

**While reading the answer:**
- Does it answer what I asked?
- Are important claims specific and concrete?
- Is uncertainty acknowledged?
- Could this be outdated?

**Before acting on the answer:**
- Verify important factual claims with independent sources.
- Check all citations before trusting them.
- For medical, legal, financial, or safety-critical matters — consult a qualified professional.

**Red flags:**
- Uniform confidence with no hedging.
- Specific-sounding citations you cannot verify.
- Vague generalities in place of concrete answers.
- Pressure to act without verification.
